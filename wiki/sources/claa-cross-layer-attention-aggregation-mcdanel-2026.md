# CLAA: Cross-Layer Attention Aggregation for Accelerating LLM Prefill

**Bradley McDanel, Steven Li, Harshit Khaitan** (Meta Reality Labs; McDanel also Franklin and Marshall College) — [arXiv:2602.16054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16054), cs.CL, v1 17 Feb 2026. Preprint, no venue given ("15 pages, 8 figures").

> [!note] Classification
> Although ingested alongside the sparse-*attention* prefill papers, CLAA is a **token-pruning** (token-ranking) method in the GemFilter / FastKV / Speculative Prefill lineage. It ranks prompt tokens, **drops the rest from the hidden-state sequence at a pruning layer** and **evicts them from the KV cache at every layer after the first four**. It therefore cuts the attention *and* the projection/FFN work for the pruned layers, which is the opposite trade from [[minference-dynamic-sparse-attention-jiang-2024|MInference]] and [[token-sparse-attention-jo-2026|Token Sparse Attention]], which keep every token and sparsify only the attention matrix. The three are orthogonal and can compose.

## Summary

The paper's first contribution is an evaluation instrument, not a speedup. Token-ranking heuristics accelerate prefill by running the expensive part of the forward pass on only the top-k% of prompt tokens, but each one derives its ranking from a different signal — GemFilter from the *last prompt token's* pre-softmax scores at one routing layer, FastKV from a *window of the W most recent prompt tokens'* post-softmax attention at one "token-selective propagation" layer, Speculative Prefill from the queries of *k lookahead tokens generated by a small draft model*, max-pooled over the draft's layers and heads — and end-to-end benchmarks conflate ranking quality with architecture. The **Answer-Informed Oracle** defines ground-truth importance after the fact: generate the full answer with the same model, then score each prompt token by the attention it receives from the answer's queries (max over layers and heads, mean over answer tokens, 1D average-pooled). Comparing heuristics to the oracle by Spearman rank correlation, layer by layer on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, exposes what the benchmarks hide: FastKV and GemFilter correlations swing sharply from layer to layer with troughs at specific layers (around layer 10 on NarrativeQA, repeated on Qasper, HotpotQA, QMSum), and the early layers (0–4, more broadly 0–8) are unreliable for every method. A single-layer heuristic that happens to prune at a trough silently loses important tokens.

**Cross-Layer Attention Aggregation (CLAA)** is the minimal fix: don't trust one layer. It uses FastKV's scoring signal — softmax attention from an observation window of W = 8 recent prompt tokens, summed over heads (Eq. 5) — but (i) defers all KV compression for the first m = 4 layers so early representations form on the full context, (ii) keeps a rolling buffer of the last n = 4 layers' scores, and (iii) at the pruning layer l_p = 15 takes the **maximum** score across the buffer, so a token that any recent layer found important survives, then gathers the hidden states down to the keep rate (10%, 20% or 40%) and runs the remaining layers on the pruned sequence. Per-layer KV caches are compressed by that layer's own score. Everything runs on FlashAttention-2 without kernel changes; the W×L scoring per layer costs under 2% of TTFT.

On LongBench with LLaMA-3.1-8B at 10% keep, CLAA scores 47.13 against the oracle's 47.83 and FullKV's 49.32, versus FastKV 46.81, Speculative Prefill 41.99 (with a Llama-3.2-1B drafter) and GemFilter 37.59; CLAA at 20% (48.12) beats FastKV at 40% (47.68). At 64K on RULER (40% keep) it reaches 82.01 vs. FastKV 81.28 and FullKV 83.78, with the largest gains on retrieval (89.85) and multi-hop (87.72); on Needle-in-a-Haystack it is the only heuristic without positional blind spots (average 0.909). TTFT on a 10K-token prompt falls from roughly 900 ms to 550 ms (**−39%**) at 10% keep on an A100 with HF Transformers, and against prompt-rewriting compressors (LLMLingua, Selective Context) it is 1.34–1.45× faster than FullKV where they are often *slower* than FullKV. The honest caveats: margins over FastKV are 0.3–0.8 points and FastKV wins three of the nine model×keep-rate cells; summarization tasks show flat correlations for every heuristic, which the authors read as a fundamental limit of committing to one ranking before generation; and it is single-turn only.

## Key points

**Where this sits: token pruning, so the end-to-end gain is not bounded by attention's share.** With 10% keep at layer 15 of 32, layers 16–31 — half the model — process a tenth of the tokens, so roughly 45% of the projection/FFN FLOPs disappear along with the corresponding attention. That is why CLAA can report a 39% TTFT reduction on a **10K-token** prompt, a length at which attention is only about a quarter of an 8B model's prefill FLOPs (derived: 4·N²·d·L ≈ 5×10¹⁴ vs. 2·N·params ≈ 1.6×10¹⁵) and at which pure attention sparsifiers show no gain at all (Token Sparse Attention ×0.97 at 8K; MInference's own limitation note says latency "approaches FlashAttention" at 10K). The paper reports **no attention-kernel speedup**, only end-to-end TTFT, and the absolute numbers are on HuggingFace Transformers rather than vLLM/[[paged-attention]], so they are for relative comparison. The price is the one the attention-sparsifiers avoid: pruned tokens are gone for the rest of prefill and for decode (the KV cache is compressed to the keep rate), so the method is lossy on any task where importance shifts during generation.

**The heuristics, formalised** (§2.2–2.3; the paper's Eq. 1–4):

| Method | Query source for scoring | Softmax? | Where it decides | Architecture | KV cache |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GemFilter | last prompt token's query at routing layer r | no (raw scores) | one layer (r = 15) | two passes: partial pass to rank, then full pass on pruned tokens with original position ids | one shared subset; implementation retains full cache and indexes into it |
| FastKV | W = 8 most recent prompt tokens at TSP layer | yes | one layer (l_TSP = 15) | single pass; per-layer KV compression, one-time sequence pruning at l_TSP | per-layer, per KV-head-group compression |
| Speculative Prefill | k = 8 lookahead tokens generated by a 1B draft model, max over draft layers/heads | no | draft model | two models: draft prefill + draft generation, then base prefill on pruned tokens | one shared subset |
| Answer-Informed Oracle | every generated answer token's query, max over layers/heads, mean over answer | no | after generation | offline ranking, then pruned prefill mirroring the evaluated heuristic's architecture | upper bound |
| **CLAA** | W = 8 recent prompt tokens, max over the last n = 4 layers | yes | window of layers ending at l_p = 15, none before m = 4 | single pass (FastKV architecture) | per-layer compression; first 4 layers uncompressed |

All methods use 1D average pooling with kernel 7 on scores, greedy decoding, the same keep rate for prefill pruning and decode-time KV compression, one A100 80GB, FlashAttention-2.

**Ranking quality against the oracle** (Fig. 2, App. C): single-layer heuristics reach correlations comparable to CLAA in deep layers (20–31) but pass through troughs on the way; CLAA's curve has no troughs. The gap that remains between CLAA and the oracle is "the cost of causal prediction": on TriviaQA, where the query names the entity, CLAA (92.37) matches the oracle (91.43) at 10% keep; on Qasper the gap persists (42.36 vs. 43.94). Speculative Prefill's lookahead should in principle close that gap, but a 1B drafter predicts the 8B model's attention poorly, and it lands at 41.99 vs. CLAA's 47.13.

The autoregressive rewrite dominates the compressors' latency; they would only pay off when one rewritten prompt is reused across queries. Note the text's "within 4% of the baseline" holds for Qasper (−1.7) and TriviaQA (0.0) but not GovReport (0.377 → 0.310, −6.7 points absolute).

**Ablations** (Fig. 6–7, App. B): pruning later is monotonically better for every heuristic (FastKV 32.5% at layer 3 → 46.5% at layer 15) and all converge toward the oracle in late layers, so l_p = 15 is a compute/accuracy compromise, not an optimum; CLAA's advantage is largest at *early* pruning layers. Widening the aggregation window from n = 1 to n = 2 gives the main boost; 20%/40% keep stabilise around n = 4, while 10% keep is volatile and peaks at n = 2. Deferring compression to m = 4 beats m = 0 at every keep rate. Hyperparameter grid: l_p ∈ {3, 7, 11, 15, 19}, n ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 12}, m ∈ {0, 2, 4, 6}, W = 8, pooling 7.

**Where it breaks**

- *Summarization*: Multi-News-style tasks give flat oracle correlations for all methods; importance evolves during generation, so any prefill-time ranking is "fundamentally limited" (authors' words). On GovReport CLAA (20%) loses 6.7 points.
- *Multi-turn*: rankings from turn 1 need not hold later; re-ranking each turn erodes the gain. Untested.
- *Aggressive keep rates*: at 10% the window ablation is unstable and retrieval drops most; the reported 39% TTFT cut is at this most fragile setting.
- *Lossy by construction*: unlike the attention-sparsifiers, dropped tokens do not come back — the KV cache for decode is the pruned one.
- *Small margins*: 0.3–0.8 points over FastKV on LongBench; the larger win is the oracle framework and the NIAH/RULER retrieval robustness, not raw accuracy.
- *Measurement*: TTFT on HF Transformers, single prompt length (10K) for the latency study; no long-context latency numbers at 64K–128K where the token-pruning FLOP argument would matter most.
